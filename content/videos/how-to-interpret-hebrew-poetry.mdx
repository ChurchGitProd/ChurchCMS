## Lecture Overview: How to Interpret Hebrew Poetry

In this expository lecture, Dr. Charles Vance presents a scholarly methodology for interpreting the poetic literature of the Old Testament. Unlike Western poetry, which often relies on meter and phonetic rhyme, Biblical Hebrew poetry operates through the rhythm of ideas and semantic parallelism.

### Key Hermeneutical Principles

#### 1. The Mechanics of Parallelism (*Parallelismus Membrorum*)

Identified formally by Bishop Robert Lowth in the 18th century, Hebrew poetic units consist of balanced bicola or tricola:

* **Synonymous Parallelism**: The second line repeats the central thought of the first using different vocabulary (e.g., Psalm 19:1 — *"The heavens declare the glory of God, and the sky above proclaims his handiwork"*).
* **Antithetic Parallelism**: The second line contrasts with the first to reinforce an ethical or theological principle (e.g., Proverbs 10:1 — *"A wise son makes a glad father, but a foolish son is a sorrow to his mother"*).
* **Synthetic (Progressive) Parallelism**: The second colon carries forward, completes, or elevates the proposition stated in the first.

#### 2. Metaphorical Density and Emotional Realism

Hebrew poets deploy vivid pastoral, military, and astronomical imagery. Interpreters must avoid wooden literalism while remaining grounded in the concrete historical world of Ancient Israel.

### Recommended Study Verses

* **Psalm 1**: The contrast of the righteous tree planted by streams and the chaff driven by wind.
* **Psalm 23**: The sovereign pastoral shepherd providing in the presence of adversaries.
* **Job 38–41**: The whirlwind speeches exhibiting sublime cosmological poetry.
